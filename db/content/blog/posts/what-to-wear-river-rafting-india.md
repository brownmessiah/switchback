Getting your kit right before you step into a raft makes a genuine difference to your day on the water — comfort, safety, and how well you recover once you're back on dry land. This guide walks you through exactly what to wear for [river rafting](/activities/rafting) in India, broken down by season and water type, so there are no cold surprises.

## Why Cotton and Jeans Will Ruin Your Day

This is the single most important rule and it's worth saying first: leave cotton at home. Cotton absorbs water, stays wet for hours, and turns cold fast — even in warm conditions, being soaked in wet cotton against your skin while sitting in a moving raft will make you miserable by kilometre two. Jeans are the worst offender: heavy when dry, nearly impossible to move in when wet, and they take forever to dry.

What you want instead is anything labelled quick-dry, synthetic, or moisture-wicking — polyester, nylon, or lycra-blend sports clothing. The kind of thing you'd wear to the gym or for a run works perfectly. It gets wet, yes, but it dries on your body within minutes once you're out of the water.

## What to Wear in Summer (April to June)

Summer is when most beginners discover rafting, and conditions are forgiving in one important way: you probably won't mind getting wet. The air temperature is hot, the sun is strong, and a splash from the rapids can feel genuinely refreshing.

For these months, keep your clothing light:

- Quick-dry shorts or three-quarter length sport trousers
- A synthetic or polyester T-shirt or rash guard
- Sport sandals with a secure heel strap, or old closed shoes you don't mind soaking — never flip-flops, which come off in currents and can be hazardous
- A cap or buff that you can secure under your helmet strap, not just sitting loose on your head

Apply sunscreen generously and bring it in a small waterproof pouch if you're doing a long stretch — river reflections amplify UV exposure significantly, and the back of your neck and the tops of your feet are easy to forget.

Sunglasses are useful but they must have a strap or retainer cord. Loose eyewear goes into the river and stays there.

## What to Wear in the Shoulder Seasons (September to November, February to March)

These months bring cooler mornings, pleasant afternoon temperatures, and some of the best water conditions of the year. The air is comfortable, but the water is cooler than in peak summer, and a dunking feels sharper.

Add a layer:

- A thin synthetic long-sleeve layer or a light rash guard under a T-shirt
- Quick-dry trousers rather than shorts if you run cold
- Consider a splash jacket — a lightweight, water-resistant windbreaker designed for paddle sports — especially on morning runs when the air temperature is lowest

Many operators renting gear at popular rafting destinations have splash jackets available, so ask when you book rather than buying one outright unless you plan to raft regularly.

## What to Wear on High-Altitude and Snowmelt Rivers (Ladakh and Similar)

If you're heading to Ladakh or any high-altitude river where the water is direct snowmelt, the calculus changes entirely. The air may be sunny and the sky perfectly blue, but the water temperature in these rivers is genuinely cold — cold enough that immersion without the right gear becomes a serious concern rather than an inconvenience.

For these conditions, a wetsuit is not optional, it's sensible. Wetsuits work by trapping a thin layer of water between the neoprene and your skin; your body warms that water and it insulates you. A 3mm wetsuit is typically sufficient for summer snowmelt rivers; a 5mm wetsuit or a wetsuit combined with a thermal underlayer is better in spring.

Most operators running high-altitude routes either include or rent wetsuits as part of the package. Confirm this when you book — if they don't offer one, ask where to rent locally or reconsider whether this specific operator is equipped for cold-water conditions.

Over the wetsuit, wear a splash jacket to cut wind and water entry at the neck and wrists. Neoprene booties are worth considering too — cold feet are distracting and uncomfortable over a long run.

## Safety Gear: What the Operator Provides

Your operator provides the two pieces of mandatory safety equipment: the personal flotation device (PFD, commonly called a life jacket) and the helmet. Both should be rated and inspected before each use.

Do not bring your own PFD or helmet unless you are a trained paddler who knows exactly what standard you need for the river grade you're running. An unrated or ill-fitting life jacket is not better than none — it gives false confidence. A good operator, including the KYC-verified operators listed on [rafting in Rishikesh](/adventure/rafting-in-rishikesh) and other Outvers destinations, will fit you properly and brief you on how to use the equipment.

If a helmet or life jacket is handed to you without explanation, ask. Any operator worth your booking will take five minutes to explain the fit and the safety drill.

## What Not to Bring on the Raft

The river takes things and does not give them back. Before you climb in:

- Leave your phone ashore or in a waterproof dry bag — not in your pocket, not in your sports bra, not tucked anywhere on your person
- Remove all loose jewellery, including earrings and rings that can come off
- Leave your wallet in the vehicle or at the campsite
- Don't carry a regular backpack; if you need to carry something, use a properly sealed dry bag designed for water

Operators at most rafting put-ins have lockers or a secure drop-off point. Use them.

## The One Thing People Always Forget: Dry Clothes for After

You will be wet. Plan for it. Pack a dry change of clothes and leave it in the vehicle or at the end point — a dry T-shirt, dry trousers, and dry footwear. After an hour or two on the water, getting into dry clothes feels disproportionately good, and it's especially important if you're continuing to travel, driving back, or heading to a higher-altitude camp in the evening where temperatures drop fast.

A small towel and a light fleece or sweatshirt for after the run are worth adding to that dry bag.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I wear swimwear instead of sports clothing?

A swimsuit or boardshorts are fine as a base layer in summer, but wear a quick-dry T-shirt or rash guard over them. Most river stretches in India involve some amount of hiking to and from the put-in and take-out, and you'll be more comfortable with a little more coverage. Bikini tops alone are not recommended — they don't stay secure during a capsize.

### Do I need to buy a wetsuit before going rafting in Ladakh?

Not necessarily. Most reputable operators running cold-water or high-altitude routes either include a wetsuit in the package or rent one. Check at booking time. If an operator is running cold snowmelt rivers without offering any thermal protection, treat that as a red flag about their overall safety standards.

### Is it safe to wear contact lenses while rafting?

Possible, but uncomfortable and risky. Contacts can be dislodged by water impact or spray, and river water is not something you want in your eyes afterward. Prescription sports goggles or simply going without are both better options. If you do wear contacts, bring solution and a case in your dry bag.

### What shoe size or type works best on the raft?

You want something that stays on your foot if you go overboard: a sport sandal with a full heel strap, or an old pair of closed trainers or water shoes you're happy to soak. Many people keep a dedicated pair of old trainers specifically for water activities. Slip-ons, flip-flops, and bare feet are all hazards both in the boat and on the rocky riverbank.

Packing right is a small investment that pays off in a genuinely better experience on the water.

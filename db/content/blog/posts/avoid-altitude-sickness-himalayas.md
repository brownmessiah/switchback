Altitude sickness has ended Himalayan trips for fit, well-prepared travellers and left others completely unaffected. Understanding why it happens and what to do about it is the single most important thing you can do before heading into high country like [Leh-Ladakh](/destinations/leh-ladakh) or [Spiti Valley](/destinations/spiti). This guide covers what acute mountain sickness actually is, how to prevent it, what to watch for, and the lines you absolutely must not cross.

## What Altitude Sickness Is — and Who It Hits

Acute mountain sickness, almost always called AMS, is your body's reaction to lower oxygen levels at altitude. As you climb higher, atmospheric pressure drops and each breath delivers less oxygen. Your body compensates over time — producing more red blood cells, adjusting breathing patterns — but that process takes days, not hours.

The critical point: AMS can affect anyone above roughly 2,500–3,000 metres, regardless of how fit you are. Marathon runners get it. Twenty-five-year-olds get it. People who were fine last year get it this year. Fitness reduces your exertion at altitude, but it does not accelerate your body's adjustment to lower oxygen. Age, gender, and prior altitude experience are weak predictors. There is no reliable way to know in advance how your body will respond.

Symptoms of AMS typically appear within six to twelve hours of arriving at altitude and include:

- Headache (the most reliable early warning)
- Nausea or loss of appetite
- Fatigue and general weakness
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Disturbed or poor-quality sleep

Mild AMS is uncomfortable but manageable if you respond correctly. The danger is when people push through symptoms and continue ascending.

## The Two Severe Forms You Must Know About

HAPE (high-altitude pulmonary oedema) and HACE (high-altitude cerebral oedema) are rare but life-threatening. HAPE is fluid accumulation in the lungs; HACE is swelling of the brain. Both are medical emergencies.

Warning signs of HAPE include breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough (sometimes producing frothy or pink sputum), and an inability to walk at a normal pace on flat ground. HACE can present as severe headache unresponsive to painkillers, confusion, loss of coordination, or drowsiness progressing to inability to stay awake.

If you or anyone in your group shows these signs, descend immediately. Do not wait for morning. Do not hope it improves overnight. Descent is the treatment. Every hundred metres down matters. Get to lower altitude and medical help as fast as possible.

## The Ascent Strategy That Actually Works

The single most effective prevention for AMS is simple in principle and hard in practice: go up slowly. The widely used guideline is "climb high, sleep low" — meaning you can make day trips to higher elevations, but return to a lower altitude to sleep. Your body acclimatizes during rest, not during exertion.

For a high-altitude destination like Leh at roughly 3,500 metres, practical steps are:

1. On arrival day, do almost nothing. Rest at the guesthouse. Let your body register the altitude before you make demands on it.
2. Take a full rest and acclimatization day before travelling to anything higher — Khardung La, Pangong Lake, the Nubra Valley, or onward to higher passes.
3. If driving up from Manali through Rohtang and Baralacha La, that multi-day road journey itself provides gradual acclimatization — use it consciously rather than rushing.
4. When adding altitude on subsequent days, try to limit the gain to no more than 300–500 metres per sleeping altitude above 3,000 metres.

Flying directly into Leh gives you none of this gradual adjustment. You step off the plane already at 3,500 metres. That first day must be treated with genuine respect — no sightseeing walks, no luggage-hauling up stairs, no celebratory drinks.

## Hydration, Food, and What to Avoid

Staying well hydrated helps your body manage altitude, though it is not a cure. Aim for at least 3–4 litres of water a day in high country, more if you are trekking. Urine colour is a useful guide — pale yellow is the target.

Alcohol dehydrates you, suppresses your breathing response during sleep, and makes symptoms worse. Avoid it entirely for the first two to three days, and drink only in moderation thereafter if you must. The same applies to sleep aids and sedatives — they suppress breathing at night, exactly when you need your body working to maintain oxygen levels.

Eat adequately even if your appetite is reduced. Carbohydrate-rich meals are generally easier to process at altitude than heavy protein or fat. Ginger tea, widely available across Ladakh and Spiti guesthouses, helps with nausea.

Heavy exertion in the first day or two is a common mistake. The temptation after months of planning is to immediately explore. Resist it.

## Diamox — and Why You Should Ask a Doctor, Not the Internet

Acetazolamide, sold as Diamox, is a prescription medication that some travellers use as a preventive for AMS. It works by stimulating faster, deeper breathing, which helps raise blood oxygen levels.

Some doctors recommend it for people with a history of altitude problems or those flying directly into high-altitude destinations with minimal time to acclimatize. It is not appropriate for everyone — it has real side effects (including increased urination, tingling in the hands, and sensitivity to sunlight), and it is not suitable for people with certain allergies or medical conditions.

If you are considering it, have the conversation with a doctor before your trip — not a pharmacist counter, not a travel forum thread. A doctor who knows your health history can give you a recommendation that is actually relevant to you. Diamox does not replace acclimatization. It is an adjunct, not a solution.

This post is not medical advice. It is background to help you ask better questions.

## The Golden Rule and When to Descend

The cardinal rule of altitude medicine: **never ascend with symptoms of AMS**. If your headache has not resolved, if you feel nauseous, if you slept badly and feel worse than you did yesterday — do not go higher. Take a rest day at the same altitude and reassess.

If symptoms worsen despite resting at the same altitude, or if any HAPE or HACE warning signs appear, descend. Descend by at least 300–500 metres and ideally more. Do not bargain with altitude. The mountain is not going anywhere; you can return when you are well.

For travellers going into remote areas of [Spiti Valley](/destinations/spiti) — where distances to medical care are long and evacuation is slow — this discipline is not optional. Know before you leave base where the nearest clinic is, have emergency contacts accessible, and travel with someone who knows the warning signs.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does acclimatization take in Leh?

Most people need a minimum of two full days at Leh before they are ready to go significantly higher. On day one, rest. On day two, short gentle walks around town at the same altitude. By day three, most people can tolerate a modest ascent, though everyone's timeline differs. Do not rush this.

### Can I get altitude sickness on a trek that starts below 3,000 metres?

You can feel effects as low as 2,500 metres, particularly if you ascend quickly. Most classic Himalayan treks cross passes at 4,500–5,300 metres, where AMS risk is real. Multi-day treks with gradual profiles carry lower risk than those with sharp altitude gains. Ask your operator about the daily elevation profile before booking.

### Does being young and fit protect me?

No. Physical fitness means you can work harder at altitude without running out of breath during exertion, but it does not change how quickly your body produces the red blood cells and chemical adjustments needed to function well at high altitude. Fit young travellers are among those who get into trouble precisely because they feel capable of pushing through early symptoms.

### What should I carry in my bag for a high-altitude trip?

At minimum: a reliable headache reliever like ibuprofen or paracetamol, a thermometer, oral rehydration salts, and if prescribed by a doctor, your Diamox course. More importantly, carry the knowledge of the warning signs and a shared understanding with your group that descent is never a failure — it is the correct decision.

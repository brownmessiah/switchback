Bir is two places at once. One is the paragliding capital of India, where pilots launch off Billing ridge and thermal above a green Kangra valley. The other is a living Tibetan refugee settlement that has, over six decades, built monasteries, institutes of Buddhist philosophy, and a food culture rooted in the high Plateau. Come for one and you will almost certainly stay for both. This guide walks you through every corner of the Tibetan colony — what to see, what to eat, how to behave, and why it is worth a full day even if you never clip into a harness.

## How Bir Became a Tibetan Town

After the Dalai Lama's exile in 1959, tens of thousands of Tibetan refugees settled across northern India. The Kangra valley offered a climate not entirely unlike parts of Tibet — cool winters, open land, and a government willing to allocate space for a self-contained settlement. Bir's Tibetan colony, known locally as Chowgan Colony, grew organically around this grant. Today the colony has its own schools, clinics, handicraft cooperatives, and a cluster of monasteries that belong to different lineages of Tibetan Buddhism. Walking through it feels nothing like the hill-station towns nearby. The streets are quieter, the architecture lower and more deliberate, and the air carries the faint scent of juniper incense drifting out of doorways.

## The Monasteries Worth Slowing Down For

**Chokling Monastery** anchors the colony. Its golden stupa is the first landmark you notice coming in from the main road, and it genuinely earns a slow visit. The monastery belongs to the Nyingma lineage and houses a community of monks who follow a rigorous schedule of practice. The main temple is painted in deep ochre and burgundy, interior walls covered in intricate thangka-style murals. Spin the row of brass prayer wheels along the outer wall — always clockwise — and take a circuit of the stupa before you enter.

About four kilometres out of Bir, on a hillside above the village of Sherabling, sits **Palpung Sherabling Monastery**. This is one of the largest Kagyu monasteries in India, with a shedra (monastic college), a retreat centre, and residential quarters for hundreds of monks. The scale surprises visitors who arrive expecting something modest. The main assembly hall has a high gilded ceiling, floor cushions arranged in long rows, and butter lamps burning in banks along the altar. If you arrive when a puja is in progress, sit quietly at the back. The low, rhythmic chanting can feel genuinely transformative even if you have no background in Buddhist practice.

**Dzongsar Khyentse Chokyi Lodro Institute**, usually called the Dzongsar Institute, is a shedra for higher Buddhist studies in the Sakya tradition. It is less visited than the other two, which makes it quieter and, in some ways, more rewarding. The courtyard has a courtyard mural programme that is still being completed, and the library holds rare Tibetan texts. This is a working academic institution — treat it accordingly.

## Prayer Flags, Stupas, and the Language of the Colony

You do not need to visit every monastery to feel the Tibetan character of Bir. It is in the details. Strings of lung ta — the multicoloured prayer flags printed with the Wind Horse — connect rooftops, trees, and telephone poles throughout the colony. Tsha-tsha clay stupas are placed in niches along walls. Mani stones carved with the mantra **Om Mani Padme Hum** are stacked near pathways. The correct etiquette around all of these is the same: pass to the left, moving clockwise. This applies to stupas, chortens, prayer-wheel walls, and monasteries. It is not superstition; it is respect for a living tradition.

Inside monasteries: remove your shoes at the entrance, keep your voice low, and do not point your feet toward the altar. If you want to photograph monks or the interior of a temple, ask first and accept a no gracefully. Many practitioners consider cameras disruptive during pujas, and some spaces are simply not meant to be recorded.

## The Deer Park Institute and Buddhist Learning

A short walk from the Tibetan colony is the **Deer Park Institute**, a centre for Buddhist studies and contemplative arts that hosts teachers from various traditions. It runs programmes in philosophy, meditation, and Tibetan language, and its library and lecture hall are open to serious visitors. Even if you are not attending a course, the grounds are peaceful and the notice board gives you a sense of the intellectual life that runs through Bir alongside the adventure economy. The Institute has hosted some of the most prominent Buddhist teachers alive today.

## Tibetan Food: What to Eat and Where

The colony's cafes and small restaurants serve some of the most authentic Tibetan food in India outside Dharamsala, and the prices are modest. Here is what to order:

- **Momos** — steamed or fried dumplings, usually filled with vegetables or minced meat, served with a red chilli sauce. Every cafe has them. Quality varies; ask locals which stall they use.
- **Thukpa** — a clear or slightly spiced noodle soup, deeply restorative after a cold morning walk. The broth is typically made from bone or vegetables with hand-pulled noodles.
- **Tingmo** — a steamed bread with a pull-apart spiral texture, eaten with lentil soup or butter. Soft, filling, and hard to stop eating.
- **Butter tea** (po cha) — salted, churned with yak butter, and an acquired taste for most Indian travellers. Try it once. It is not a sweet beverage; think of it as a warm, savoury drink that makes sense at altitude.
- **Sweet milk tea** — if po cha is not for you, the Tibetan community also makes a gentler sweet chai that tastes familiar and excellent.

Budget roughly ₹150–₹400 for a filling meal at colony restaurants, though confirm current prices directly with any restaurant as costs shift with seasons and supply. Bir also has some newer cafes catering to the paragliding crowd — good coffee, but the Tibetan colony is where the real food is.

## Tibetan Handicrafts and What to Bring Home

The colony has small shops and a handicraft cooperative selling items made by the community. Thangka paintings, hand-knotted carpets, singing bowls, prayer beads (mala), incense, and small ritual objects are available. Prices are fair by hill-station standards and buying here supports the settlement directly rather than a wholesale distributor. If you are buying a thangka, look for work that is clearly hand-painted rather than printed — the quality difference shows in the brushwork on fine details. Pieces can range from a few hundred rupees for a small printed scroll to several thousand for a genuine painted thangka, depending on size and complexity.

## Pairing Culture with Adventure

[Bir Billing](/destinations/bir-billing) is compact enough that you can spend the morning at Chokling Monastery, eat thukpa for lunch, walk through the colony in the afternoon, and still have time to watch the sunset from a hilltop with a cup of sweet tea. Many visitors who come specifically for [paragliding in Bir Billing](/adventure/paragliding-in-bir-billing) find the cultural layer of the trip becomes the part they talk about most when they get home. The two sides of Bir do not compete — they complement each other in a way that is rare among Indian adventure destinations.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do I need to see the main monasteries in Bir?

A relaxed half-day covers Chokling Monastery and a walk through the colony, including a stop for food. To add Palpung Sherabling you need a full day, ideally with your own vehicle or a hired local taxi since it is a few kilometres uphill from the colony.

### Is Bir Billing culturally accessible for non-Buddhist visitors?

Yes, completely. The monasteries welcome respectful visitors of any background. You do not need to know anything about Buddhism to appreciate the spaces, the art, or the community. Simply follow the basic etiquette — clockwise movement, shoes off inside, quiet during prayer — and you will be welcome.

### What is the best time of year to visit the Tibetan colony in Bir?

October to early December and March to May are the most comfortable months. Summers are pleasant and green. December through February can be cold but the monasteries are active and less crowded. The monsoon (July–August) brings leeches on walking paths but the valley turns intensely lush — trade-offs to weigh.

### Are the restaurants in the Tibetan colony vegetarian-friendly?

Largely yes, though the community does eat meat. Most cafe menus have a full vegetarian section covering momos, thukpa, tingmo, and rice dishes. Always ask if you have dietary restrictions, as the kitchen will usually accommodate you if you ask clearly.

River rafting grades are the single most useful number you will encounter when planning a whitewater trip, yet most first-timers book a run without fully understanding what they mean. This guide breaks down the International Scale of River Difficulty from Grade I through VI, anchors it in real Indian rivers you can actually raft, and gives you a clear framework for choosing a run that matches your experience — not just your appetite for adrenaline.

## What the International Scale of River Difficulty Actually Measures

The grading system was designed to describe how technically demanding and how dangerous a stretch of river is, not how exciting it looks on video. A high-grade rapid demands precise paddling, physical stamina, and the ability to self-rescue or survive a swim in fast, turbulent water. A low-grade rapid is forgiving — mistakes correct themselves and the consequences of capsizing are minor.

Six grades exist. Grades I through IV are commercially rafted with trained guides. Grade V is for expert paddlers with advanced rescue training. Grade VI is generally considered unrunnable — or runnable only by elite kayakers under ideal conditions at significant personal risk.

Every grade on this scale is also conditional. The same stretch of river can move two grades higher after heavy rain, a dam release, or snowmelt. A river rated Grade III in February can become Grade IV-plus in late June. Always ask the operator what grade the river is running at the time of your trip, not what it is on a map.

## Grade I and II: Where Every Rafter Should Begin

**Grade I** water is moving but calm — small, regular waves, no serious obstacles, minimal manoeuvring required. Think of a fast-flowing river with the occasional gentle ripple. There is almost no risk for a swimmer, and children above the minimum age limit (typically eight to ten, depending on the operator) can participate comfortably.

**Grade II** introduces you to genuine rapids: faster water, slightly irregular waves, some rocks and obstacles that require the guide to steer around them. The manoeuvres are straightforward and clearly visible from upstream. This is the level where you learn how paddle commands — "forward," "back-paddle," "stop" — actually matter.

The Brahmpuri stretch in Rishikesh is the classic Indian entry point, running Grade I to II. It is short enough for a morning outing, forgiving enough to build confidence, and gives you a real taste of the Ganga's current. The Kundalika river in Maharashtra, fed by controlled dam releases, runs at a consistent Grade II to III and is popular for day trips from Pune and Mumbai precisely because the flow is predictable and beginner-friendly.

If you have never been on a raft before, start here. There is nothing wrong with Grade II. Understanding how your body weight and paddle stroke influence the boat at low grades makes you a safer, more capable rafter when you step up.

## Grade III: The Sweet Spot for Most Recreational Rafters

**Grade III** is where recreational rafting comes into its own. Rapids are moderate to moderately large — irregular waves, narrow passages, strong eddies, and hydraulic features that require active manoeuvring. A swim is no longer consequence-free; you may be carried some distance before you can exit the current, and the guide will need to move quickly to retrieve you. That said, Grade III is commercially operated widely and safely worldwide with competent guides and proper equipment.

The Shivpuri corridor in Rishikesh is India's most-rafted Grade III stretch. It runs roughly 16 kilometres and includes named rapids — Roller Coaster, Golf Course, Club House — that are challenging enough to get your heart rate up without requiring technical expertise from the paddlers. Guides here are experienced, safety kayakers accompany commercial groups, and the volume of traffic on this corridor means rescue infrastructure is well-established.

If you have completed a Grade I–II run before, you have a reasonable baseline for Grade III. Come prepared to paddle hard on command, stay calm if the boat tilts, and follow your guide's instructions without hesitation.

## Grade IV: Advanced Water — Know What You Are Signing Up For

**Grade IV** rapids are powerful, pushy, and demanding. Waves are large and often irregular, holes and pour-overs are real hazards, and manoeuvring must be precise and committed. A swimming exit from a Grade IV rapid is a serious situation — currents are strong, recirculating features can hold a swimmer, and self-rescue requires both skill and composure. Commercial Grade IV operations use experienced guides, mandatory safety briefings, and rigorous equipment checks for good reason.

The Shivpuri run grades into IV territory at higher water levels. The Zanskar river in Ladakh, which cuts through one of the most dramatic gorges in the subcontinent, tops out at Grade IV and is one of the more committing multi-day river journeys in India. The combination of altitude (above 3,000 metres in sections), cold water, and remote access means that operator verification and safety credentials are non-negotiable here.

Grade IV is suitable for healthy adults who are strong swimmers, have prior rafting experience, and are comfortable following precise instructions under pressure. People with heart conditions, back problems, or neck injuries should consult a doctor before attempting it.

## Grade V and VI: Expert and Extreme

**Grade V** water is long, violent, and consequential. Rapids are continuous, drops are significant, and rescue from within the rapid may be impossible. Paddlers at this level are not recreational rafters — they are trained whitewater athletes with years of experience, swift water rescue certification, and the judgment to scout, portage, or walk away from a line that looks wrong on the day.

**Grade VI** is the ceiling: waterfalls, cascades, or hydraulic features that are effectively unsurvivable for most humans. The grade is sometimes applied to runs that have been completed once or twice under exceptional conditions, but commercial operations at this level do not exist.

No Indian operator should be marketing Grade V runs to general tourists. If you encounter one that does, that is itself a red flag about their safety culture.

## What to Wear, Carry, and Confirm Before Any Grade

Regardless of grade, a reputable operator will provide a **personal flotation device (PFD)**, a **helmet**, and a paddle. These are mandatory, not optional. You should also wear:

- Secure footwear that can get wet — river sandals with heel straps or old trainers, not flip-flops
- Synthetic or quick-dry clothing — avoid cotton, which stays cold when wet
- A rash guard or UV-protective layer on sunny stretches

Before you enter the water, your guide must conduct a safety briefing. This covers swim position (on your back, feet downstream), how to re-enter the raft, what to do if you fall out, and the paddle commands you will use. Do not shrug this off because you are in a hurry or feel it is aimed at beginners. Listen actively.

Confirm with the operator: the current grade of the run at that day's water level, the minimum age and weight requirements, any medical exclusions, the ratio of safety kayakers to rafts, and what the cancellation policy is if weather or conditions deteriorate.

## Choosing the Right Run for You: A Practical Summary

- **Never rafted before, or bringing children:** Grade I–II, Brahmpuri (Rishikesh) or Kundalika (Maharashtra)
- **Fit adults, some outdoor experience, want a genuine challenge:** Grade III, Shivpuri run via [rafting in Rishikesh](/adventure/rafting-in-rishikesh)
- **Experienced rafters, strong swimmers, want multi-day adventure:** Grade IV, Zanskar or high-water Shivpuri with a fully verified operator
- **Not sure which grade suits you:** ask the operator honestly — a good guide will tell you the truth rather than upsell you to a harder run

Grade is about difficulty, not bravado. The Shivpuri run at Grade III will give you more genuine whitewater experience than you can absorb on a first trip. Work your way up methodically, and the sport rewards you with increasingly extraordinary rivers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Grade III rafting safe for someone who cannot swim?

Grade III carries real swimming risk — if you fall out of the raft you may be in fast water for a meaningful stretch of time. Non-swimmers can participate if the operator explicitly permits it, but you should be honest about your ability at the time of booking, follow every safety instruction without exception, and consider starting with Grade I–II first. The PFD will keep you on the surface, but the ability to stay calm and follow the guide's recovery instructions is essential.

### How much does a rafting trip in India typically cost?

Pricing varies by river, distance, and season. A short Grade II–III day run in Rishikesh typically ranges from approximately ₹600 to ₹1,500 per person; multi-day expeditions on remote rivers like the Zanskar can cost several thousand rupees per day once camping, meals, and permits are included. Always confirm the current price directly with the operator at the time of booking — seasonal demand and permit costs shift pricing regularly.

### Can grades change on the same river between visits?

Yes, significantly. Snow-fed rivers in Uttarakhand and Ladakh rise sharply in May and June as temperatures climb and melt accelerates. The monsoon (roughly July to mid-September) raises most Himalayan rivers by multiple grades and suspends commercial operations on many stretches entirely. The safest windows for most Indian rafting corridors are February to April and September to November. Ask the operator about current conditions before you commit.

Look for guides who hold recognised whitewater rescue training certificates, safety kayakers accompanying the commercial raft, mandatory briefings, and equipment that is visually inspected before launch.

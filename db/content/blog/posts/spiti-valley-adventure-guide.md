Spiti Valley is one of the few places in India where the landscape stops you mid-sentence. A cold high-altitude desert sitting between 3,000 and 4,500 metres in Himachal Pradesh, it rewards those who put in the effort to reach it with monasteries perched on crumbling cliffs, villages that feel untouched by the last few centuries, and some of the most demanding adventure terrain on the subcontinent. This guide covers everything you need to plan a safe, well-timed, genuinely memorable trip — routes in, highlights, adventure options, and the honest realities of travelling here.

## Two routes in, one very different experience

Getting to Spiti is part of the journey. There are two approaches, and your choice shapes the entire trip.

The **Shimla–Kinnaur route** via NH-5 is open year-round (weather permitting), and it is the more reliable option if you are travelling outside peak summer. The road follows the Sutlej river through Kinnaur before climbing into Spiti, and the gradient is gradual enough for your body to acclimatise naturally. Reckon on two days of driving from Shimla to Kaza, the main town in Spiti, with a night in Nako or Tabo breaking the journey sensibly.

The **Manali–Rohtang–Kunzum Pass route** is the dramatic approach — crossing two high passes, with Kunzum La sitting above 4,500 metres. It is open roughly June to October and often closes earlier than expected due to early snowfall. The views are extraordinary, but the altitude gain is fast, and altitude sickness is a real risk if you push straight through from Manali without stopping. Many travellers combine both routes: enter via Manali in summer and exit via Kinnaur, or vice versa.

**Practical point:** carry enough cash before leaving Shimla or Manali. ATMs exist in Kaza but run out regularly, and smaller villages have none. Do not assume a UPI connection either — mobile data is patchy and often absent.

## Acclimatisation is not optional

People underestimate altitude here. Kaza sits at around 3,800 metres. Kibber, Komic, and Hikkim go higher. The headache, nausea, and fatigue of acute mountain sickness can turn a dream trip into a miserable or dangerous one very quickly.

The standard advice holds: ascend slowly, drink water constantly, avoid alcohol for the first few days, and do not push hard on the first day at altitude. If you are flying into Chandigarh or Delhi and driving straight up, plan a full rest day in Kaza before attempting higher villages or treks. Descend immediately if symptoms worsen — no view is worth your safety.

Children, elderly travellers, and anyone with cardiac or respiratory conditions should speak to a doctor before planning this trip. It does not suit everyone, and that is not a reason to feel discouraged — it is a reason to plan honestly.

## Key monastery, Tabo, and Dhankar — the cultural core

Spiti's monasteries are not tourist attractions in the conventional sense. They are living institutions, some over a thousand years old, still functioning as centres of Tibetan Buddhist practice.

**Key Monastery** (Ki Gompa) is the largest in Spiti, dramatically positioned above the Spiti river valley. It houses hundreds of monks, an impressive collection of thangkas and weapons, and offers some of the best views in the region. Arrive respectfully — dress modestly, remove shoes where asked, and do not photograph monks or prayer sessions without permission.

**Tabo Monastery**, founded in 996 CE, is sometimes called the Ajanta of the Himalayas for its ancient murals and stucco sculptures. It is a UNESCO tentative list site and genuinely irreplaceable. Take your time here; the art rewards slow looking.

**Dhankar**, perched vertiginously above the confluence of the Spiti and Pin rivers, is one of the most photogenic fortresses in the Himalayas. The old monastery here is structurally fragile — walk carefully, follow any guidance from the caretakers, and treat it gently.

**Hikkim** deserves a mention simply because it holds what is reportedly one of the world's highest functioning post offices. Sending a postcard from here to a friend back home is a small, genuine pleasure.

## High-altitude trekking in Spiti

[Spiti treks](/activities/trekking) sit in a different category from most Himalayan routes. You are not in lush forest; you are in an open, rocky, sparsely vegetated landscape where navigation is harder, weather changes fast, and evacuation in an emergency takes time. That is not a reason to avoid trekking here — it is a reason to prepare properly and go with a guide or organised group if you are not highly experienced.

Popular routes include the trek from Kibber to Tashigang and on to Losar, passing through remote high-altitude grazing land. The Pin–Parvati Pass trek connects Pin Valley with Kullu, and is considered one of the tougher crossings in Himachal — technically demanding with glacial sections, suited to experienced trekkers with appropriate gear. The Chandratal circuit, taking in the lake at around 4,250 metres, is among the most accessible multi-day options and stunning in its own right.

What to carry: layered clothing (temperatures can drop below freezing even in July at altitude), a reliable sleeping bag rated to at least -10°C for high camps, sunscreen rated SPF 50 or higher (the UV intensity at altitude is punishing), and a basic first-aid kit that includes diamox if your doctor recommends it. Check with your operator about what is provided versus what you need to bring.

## Camping under some of the clearest skies in India

[High-altitude camping](/activities/camping) in Spiti is transformative. Light pollution is almost non-existent across most of the valley, and on a clear night the Milky Way is simply overwhelming. Organised camping in Spiti typically runs from roughly ₹1,000 to ₹2,500 per person per night depending on the setup — basic tents at the lower end, more comfortable setups with proper bedding and meals included at the higher end. Always confirm the current price directly with your operator before booking, as costs vary with season and group size.

Chandratal is the obvious camping destination — the lake's turquoise colour and the surrounding peaks make for one of the most beautiful campsites anywhere in India. Note that camping directly on the lakeshore is now restricted to protect the ecology; your operator will know the current permitted zones.

If you are camping independently, carry out everything you bring in. The Spiti ecosystem is fragile — slow to recover from damage at altitude and in low-rainfall desert conditions. This is not a place to leave any trace.

## Winter travel and snow leopard spotting near Kibber

Most people visit Spiti in summer, but winter (roughly December to February) offers something genuinely rare: the chance of seeing snow leopards near Kibber village. The cats come lower in search of prey during the coldest months, and local wildlife guides have developed reasonable track records for spotting them on the rocky slopes above the village.

Winter travel requires serious preparation. The Manali route is closed. The Kinnaur route is nominally open but subject to closure after heavy snowfall. Temperatures drop well below -20°C at night in the upper valley. You need properly insulated accommodation, the right clothing, and a clear understanding that infrastructure is minimal. Several operators run dedicated winter snow leopard camps near Kibber — book well in advance and be honest with yourself about your cold-weather tolerance.

## Responsible travel in a fragile place

Spiti's communities are small, infrastructure is under stress, and the landscape is genuinely vulnerable to the kind of careless behaviour that has damaged other popular Himalayan destinations.

A few things that matter:

- Stay in locally owned guesthouses and camps where possible. Money spent here directly supports the communities you are visiting.
- Do not collect rocks, plants, or any natural material. It seems obvious; it still happens.
- Ask before photographing people, especially monks and women. A smile and a question cost nothing.
- Water is scarce. Use it accordingly.
- Carry a bag for your rubbish and use the collection points in Kaza. Do not burn plastic.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the best time to visit Spiti Valley?

June to September is the window when both entry routes are typically accessible, the weather is at its most forgiving, and the full range of trekking and camping activities is available. July and August can bring some rain even in this rain-shadow zone, but it is rarely prolonged. October sees rapid cooling and the Manali route typically closes. For snow leopard trips, December to February is the target window — but read the winter section above before committing.

### Do I need permits to travel in Spiti?

Some areas near Pin Valley National Park and close to the international border require Inner Line Permits for foreign nationals. Indian nationals generally do not need special permits for the main Spiti circuit. Requirements do change, so confirm with your operator at the time of booking what paperwork, if any, you need.

### How should I handle altitude sickness?

Take it seriously from the start. Ascend gradually — ideally one significant altitude gain per day — rest on arrival in Kaza, drink water constantly, and avoid alcohol for the first few days. If you develop a severe headache, vomiting, loss of coordination, or confusion, descend immediately and seek medical help. Diamox (acetazolamide) can help with acclimatisation for some people but consult your doctor before the trip rather than buying it in a pharmacy on the road.

### Is Spiti suitable for first-time Himalayan travellers?

Yes, with caveats. The main Spiti circuit — Kaza, Key, Kibber, Tabo, Dhankar, Chandratal — does not require technical mountaineering skills and is accessible to fit, well-prepared travellers who are careful about acclimatisation. The harder treks like Pin–Parvati Pass are not suitable for beginners. Be honest about your fitness and experience when speaking to an operator, and follow their guidance on route selection.

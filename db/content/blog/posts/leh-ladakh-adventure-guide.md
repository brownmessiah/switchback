Ladakh is one of the few places left in India that genuinely rewards preparation. Get the timing, the acclimatisation, and the permits right, and you will be rewarded with river gorges, passes above 5,000 metres, and skies you will not find anywhere else. Get them wrong and the altitude will flatten you before the adventure even begins. This guide covers everything you need to plan a real, safe, well-timed trip to [Leh-Ladakh](/destinations/leh-ladakh).

## When to Go and When to Stay Away

The adventure season runs roughly from **late May through September**. The Manali–Leh and Srinagar–Leh highways typically open between late May and early June depending on snowfall, so confirm road status before planning your drive or bike trip. July and August bring the most reliable weather for rafting, trekking, and camping, though brief afternoon showers are normal. September is often the sweetest month — crowds thin out, the light is extraordinary, and passes are still fully open.

October sees temperatures drop sharply and many guesthouses close by month-end. November through April, most of Ladakh shuts for leisure travel. The two notable exceptions are the **Chadar Trek** on the frozen Zanskar River (typically mid-January to mid-February, weather-dependent) and snow leopard tracking in Hemis National Park, which is genuinely world-class in winter. Both require serious cold-weather preparation and experienced guides — they are not casual alternatives.

## Altitude Sickness: This is the One Thing You Cannot Skip

Leh sits at roughly **3,500 metres**. The major passes — Khardung La, Chang La, Baralacha La — exceed 5,000 metres. Many travellers, including fit and experienced ones, feel altitude sickness. It is not a sign of weakness; it is physiology.

The standard approach that works:

- **Fly in, rest immediately.** If you fly into Leh, spend your first 24–48 hours doing almost nothing. Short slow walks, no exertion, no alcohol.
- **Hydrate consistently.** Aim for three to four litres of water a day. More than you think you need.
- **Ascend gradually on road trips.** If driving from Manali or Srinagar, your body acclimatises over several days — this is actually an advantage over flying.
- **Know the warning signs.** Persistent headache, nausea, confusion, and loss of coordination are serious. Descend and seek help immediately — do not push through severe symptoms.
- **Consult your doctor about Diamox.** Some travellers use acetazolamide prophylactically. Get medical advice before your trip, not after landing.

Children, older travellers, and people with cardiac or respiratory conditions should discuss Ladakh specifically with their doctor. Operators offering treks and rafting will typically ask about health conditions at booking — answer honestly, because the limits exist for good reason.

## Inner Line Permits: What You Actually Need

Several of Ladakh's most iconic destinations require an **Inner Line Permit (ILP)**. As of recent seasons, Indian nationals need permits for Nubra Valley, Pangong Tso, Tso Moriri, and Dah-Hanu. Foreign nationals require Protected Area Permits for some of these zones as well.

Permits are available online through the Ladakh Tourism portal or in person through registered travel agents in Leh. The process is straightforward and typically inexpensive — the fee is modest, but confirm current rates and requirements at the time of your trip since rules do get updated.

**Practical tip:** Apply for permits a few days before you need them. Build buffer time into your itinerary for areas like Pangong and Nubra. Permits usually cover a fixed number of days and specify the areas you can visit, so plan your route before applying rather than after.

## Adventures Worth Building Your Trip Around

**Motorbike road trips** are the iconic Ladakh experience. The Manali–Leh Highway, followed by loops through Nubra Valley and Pangong Tso, is one of the great motorcycle journeys in Asia. Rent a Royal Enfield in Leh (costs vary, confirm locally) or ride your own. Mechanical reliability matters at altitude — always travel with a basic toolkit and do not ride solo on remote stretches.

**River rafting on the Indus and Zanskar** runs from June through September. The Indus offers accessible stretches for beginners, while the Zanskar gorge produces serious whitewater in the Grade III–IV range. A day's rafting on the Zanskar typically costs in the range of **₹1,500–₹3,000 per person**, though prices vary by operator, season, and stretch. Always confirm the current price directly with the operator at the time of booking. Check what safety equipment is included — helmets, life jackets, and a qualified safety kayaker are non-negotiable minimums.

**High-altitude treks** are among the most rewarding in the Himalayas. The Markha Valley trek crosses Kongmaru La at around 5,200 metres and passes through remote villages with homestays. The Sham Valley trek (sometimes called the Baby Trek) is more accessible, sitting at lower altitudes and manageable for trekkers without previous Himalayan experience. Explore these and other [high-altitude treks](/activities/trekking) to find the right level for your fitness and experience.

**Mountain biking** has grown steadily as an activity. Routes descending from passes like Khardung La are spectacular, though the altitude and terrain demand genuine fitness and bike-handling experience. Go with a guided group unless you are an experienced off-road cyclist who has done high-altitude riding before.

**Camping at Pangong and Nubra** has its own particular magic — waking at 4,300 metres with the lake changing colour as the sun hits it is something that stays with you. Permits are required, and camp operators in these areas have become much better organised in recent seasons. Book through a verified operator rather than arranging informally, both for convenience and because permitted, responsible camping protects these fragile ecosystems.

## What to Pack (The Essentials That People Miss)

Ladakh will fool you. It can be 28°C at noon and 4°C after sunset in July. The sun at altitude is intense. Pack for a much wider temperature range than you would for most Indian hill stations.

- **Layering system:** a light base layer, a mid-layer fleece, and a down or synthetic insulated jacket. Do not skip the down.
- **Sun protection:** SPF 50+ sunscreen, UV-rated sunglasses, a wide-brimmed hat. The UV index at 3,500+ metres is serious.
- **Lip balm with SPF.** Cracked lips from dry altitude air are almost universal without it.
- **Quality water bottle or hydration bladder.** Hydration is not optional.
- **Basic first aid:** ibuprofen, rehydration salts, and any prescription medication you take.
- **Warm gloves and a woolly hat** even in peak summer, if you are planning passes or early morning activities.
- **Cash.** ATMs in Leh can run dry during peak season. Carry more than you think you need.

## Planning Your Budget

Ladakh can absorb almost any budget, from spartan homestays to high-end glamping, but do plan for a few fixed realities. Internal flights to Leh are expensive relative to other Indian domestic routes — book as far ahead as possible. Road transport is slow by design, so account for transit time in your schedule rather than cramming activities.

A realistic base budget for a week-long trip, staying in comfortable but not luxury accommodation and doing two or three paid activities, runs from roughly **₹25,000 to ₹55,000 per person** inclusive of flights, accommodation, food, permits, and one or two experiences like rafting or a guided trek day. This is a rough planning range only — costs shift meaningfully based on season, group size, accommodation tier, and how you reach Leh. Always verify current prices with operators at time of booking; the figures here are orientation, not quotes.

Group travel and early booking both reduce costs significantly. Many operators offer better rates for groups of four or more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Ladakh safe for first-time high-altitude travellers?

Yes, if you respect the acclimatisation protocol. Most altitude-related problems are preventable with two rest days in Leh, steady hydration, and not racing immediately to high passes. The serious cases almost always involve travellers who ignored symptoms or ascended too fast. Book activities with KYC-verified, experienced operators who know the environment and have safety procedures in place.

### Do I need a permit to visit Pangong Tso or Nubra Valley?

Yes. Both areas require an Inner Line Permit for Indian nationals, available online through the Ladakh Tourism portal or through registered agents in Leh. Processing is usually quick, but build in a day or two of buffer before you need to travel. Requirements do change, so confirm the current rules close to your travel date.

### What is the best way to reach Leh?

Flying from Delhi, Mumbai, or other major cities is the fastest option but is expensive and gives you the most abrupt altitude gain — plan strict rest days if you fly. The Manali–Leh Highway (typically open late May to late October) and the Srinagar–Leh Highway (slightly longer season) are spectacular road journeys that help your body acclimatise gradually. Many travellers fly in and drive out, or vice versa.

### Can I do rafting on the Zanskar without prior rafting experience?

Certain sections of the Zanskar and Indus are suitable for beginners, and a good operator will match you to a stretch appropriate for your experience level. The more challenging Grade III–IV sections of the Zanskar gorge are not suitable for complete beginners. Be honest with your operator about your experience, listen to the safety briefing without exception, and choose an operator who provides properly fitted life jackets, helmets, and a trained guide on the water.

Whether you are planning your first Himalayan adventure or returning for a deeper exploration of the region, Ladakh repays those who arrive informed and unhurried.

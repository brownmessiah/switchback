Hampta Pass sits at roughly 4,270 metres (about 14,000 feet) in Himachal Pradesh, and it earns its reputation as one of India's best beginner-to-intermediate Himalayan crossings because of one remarkable contrast: you start in the lush, green Kullu valley and emerge into the stark, moon-like terrain of Lahaul and Spiti. This guide covers everything you need — the route, the difficulty, what to pack, what it will cost, and how to stay safe — so you can step onto that trail with confidence rather than guesswork.

## Why Hampta Pass Punches Above Its Grade

Most high-altitude passes in the Himalayas demand technical climbing skills or weeks of acclimatization. Hampta Pass does not. It is graded moderate, which means a reasonably fit person who walks regularly and can handle a long, steep day has a realistic shot at completing it. That is not the same as saying it is easy. The pass-crossing day routinely runs eight to ten hours, gains significant elevation quickly, often involves a snow slope well into July, and ends with a long scree descent that will test your knees and your patience. But the reward is immediate and visceral: within a single trekking day, the vegetation behind you disappears and you are standing in a completely different Himalayan world.

This dual character — accessible entry point, genuinely dramatic payoff — is why Hampta Pass has become one of the most popular first crossover treks in India, and why operators running [trekking near Manali](/adventure/trekking-in-manali) fill up their groups fast once the season opens.

## Season, Snow, and the Monsoon Calculus

The window is mid-June to mid-September. Within that window, the experience changes considerably depending on when you go.

**Mid-June to early July** — You will almost certainly encounter snow on and near the pass. This slows the crossing, adds a slip-and-fall risk on the ascent, and makes the trail harder to read. The Kullu side is still green and largely dry. Experienced trekkers who want dramatic snow scenery and fewer crowds tend to prefer this window.

**July to mid-August** — The Kullu valley is in full monsoon. Expect rain, leeches on the lower stretches, and some trail flooding. The upside: the meadows are extraordinarily lush, wildflowers are everywhere, and the Lahaul side remains bone-dry. The contrast between the two sides is at its most extreme. Weather windows at the pass are shorter, so flexibility helps.

**Mid-August to mid-September** — Generally considered the most stable window for a first-timer. The monsoon weakens, the pass is usually snow-free, visibility is sharp, and the temperature is more forgiving at camp. Most organised groups target this period.

Whatever month you go, carry rain gear and do not treat the forecast as gospel above 3,500 metres. Conditions at the pass can shift in under an hour.

## The Route Day by Day

The standard itinerary runs four to five trekking days. Most operators drive you from [Manali](/destinations/manali) to Jobra (roughly 2,500 metres) on Day 1, shaving several kilometres of road-walking off the start.

**Day 1 — Manali to Jobra, trek to Chika (approx. 2,900 m)**
From Jobra, you follow the Rani Nallah through pine and birch forest. The trail is clear, the altitude gain is gentle, and the campsite at Chika is a good place to begin feeling how your body responds to the elevation. Trek time: around three to four hours.

**Day 2 — Chika to Balu ka Ghera (approx. 3,600 m)**
The valley opens up dramatically. You cross several streams — some require a quick wade early in the season — and pass through wide alpine meadows. Balu ka Ghera means "sandy enclosure" and the flat, boulder-strewn camp under the pass walls is unmistakable. Trek time: five to six hours. This is the last night before the crossing, so eat well, sleep early, and drink water steadily.

**Day 3 — Balu ka Ghera over Hampta Pass (4,270 m) to Shea Goru (approx. 3,800 m)**
The day that defines the trek. You start before dawn or at first light to maximise your daylight on the descent. The ascent to the pass is steep, often icy early in the season, and the altitude will make the final push feel harder than it looks on a map. At the top, the view is worth a few minutes: the green Kullu valley behind you, the brown Lahaul plateaus ahead. The descent to Shea Goru on scree is long and hard on the knees. Trekking poles are not optional here, they are a genuine aid. Total trek time: eight to ten hours.

**Day 4 — Shea Goru to Chatru (approx. 3,300 m)**
A relatively easy walk down the Lahaul side through the Chandra River valley. You may camp at Chatru or continue by vehicle, depending on your operator's itinerary.

**Optional extension — Chandratal lake**
From Chatru, a drive of roughly 65–70 kilometres takes you to Chandratal, the "Moon Lake," at about 4,300 metres. It is a high-altitude jewel and well worth the detour if your schedule and acclimatization allow. Most full itineraries build this in as a fifth day before the return to Manali.

## Difficulty: Who This Trek Is For

Hampta Pass is a solid fit for you if you exercise regularly, can handle consecutive days of walking with a daypack, and are comfortable sleeping in tents. You do not need prior Himalayan experience, though a weekend trek at lower altitude in the months before will help your body remember what sustained climbing feels like.

Be honest with yourself if you have cardiac or respiratory conditions. The pass-crossing day reaches altitudes where altitude sickness — headache, nausea, dizziness — can develop quickly in susceptible individuals. Acclimatizing in Manali for a day or two before you start is genuinely useful, not a luxury.

The trek does not suit children under roughly 12 years old (many operators set a minimum age of 14 for the pass-crossing), anyone in the later stages of pregnancy, or anyone with uncontrolled hypertension. Age and weight limits vary by operator, so confirm before booking.

## What to Pack

Getting your kit right matters more on a multi-day camping trek than almost any other travel format, because you cannot pop out to buy what you forgot.

**Clothing layers:**
- Moisture-wicking base layer (top and bottom)
- Insulating mid-layer (fleece or light down jacket)
- Waterproof outer shell (rain jacket and rain trousers)
- Warm hat, gloves, and a neck gaiter — the pass is cold even in August
- Trekking trousers (avoid denim entirely)

**Footwear:**
Sturdy, waterproof, ankle-supporting trekking shoes that are already broken in. New boots on Day 1 is a reliable path to blisters by Day 3. Gaiters help if you expect snow.

**Gear:**
- Trekking poles — strongly recommended
- A daypack (40–50 litres) for your personal gear; operators typically arrange mules or porters for group camping equipment
- Sleeping bag rated to at least -5°C
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- Sunscreen (SPF 50+) and UV-blocking sunglasses — the UV at altitude is harsh
- Personal first-aid kit including altitude sickness medication (discuss Diamox with a doctor before you go)
- Reusable water bottle and water purification tablets

Most organised treks include tents, sleeping mats, and meals. Confirm exactly what is included when you book.

## Safety, Guides, and What to Watch For

No mountain trek is without risk, and honesty about that is more useful to you than reassurance. The things that most often go wrong on Hampta Pass are:

**Altitude sickness** — ascend gradually, hydrate consistently, descend immediately if symptoms worsen after rest.

**Weather deterioration** — the pass can cloud over fast. A good guide knows when to push forward and when to wait or turn back. Trust that judgment over personal itinerary pressure.

**Stream crossings** — early in the season, meltwater crossings can be waist-deep and fast-moving. Unbuckle your pack hip belt before crossing any significant stream so you can shed it if you fall.

**Scree descent injuries** — slow, deliberate steps and trekking poles prevent most ankle rolls.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Hampta Pass suitable for first-time Himalayan trekkers?

Yes, with one condition: you need a baseline of reasonable fitness and should take acclimatization seriously. The trek is graded moderate, which is achievable without prior Himalayan experience, but the pass-crossing day is genuinely demanding at around eight to ten hours. If you have not done any multi-day trekking, a shorter practice trek in the weeks before is worth the effort.

### When exactly is the best time to do Hampta Pass?

Mid-August to mid-September is the most consistent window for stable weather, a snow-free pass, and good visibility. If you want snow on the pass and do not mind heavier monsoon rain on the Kullu approach, late June or early July works well. July and early August are perfectly feasible but come with active monsoon conditions on the lower trail.

### Do I need to obtain permits separately?

Inner Line Permits and forest permits for this region are typically arranged by your operator and are factored into the package cost. You should confirm this when booking, but for most organised treks it is not something you need to chase independently.

### Can I do Hampta Pass without a guide or organised group?

Technically yes, but the route involves multiple stream crossings, some unmarked sections near the pass, and emergency situations that are much harder to manage alone. The organised trek cost is modest relative to the safety benefit of having an experienced guide who knows the terrain and can respond if conditions turn. Solo travel at altitude without local knowledge is a risk that is hard to justify given the pricing of organised options.
